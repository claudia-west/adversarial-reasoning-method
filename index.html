The Adversarial Reasoning Method

A conceptual framework proposal emphasizing reflection and long-term pattern integration for structured clinical diagnostic reasoning.

(Not yet empirically validated. Proposed for pilot evaluation.)

Abstract

Diagnostic error remains among the most widespread and consequential sources of preventable harm in modern medicine. The relationship between diagnostic error and its causes is not very well captured by a binary split between “knowledge failures” and “reasoning failures.” A substantial body of evidence indicates that diagnostic accuracy rests primarily on the availability of relevant knowledge in memory rather than on the reasoning strategy applied to it (Schmidt & Mamede, 2015; Norman et al., 2024; Pelaccia et al., 2025). This paper does not treat the reasoning process and knowledge as competing explanations for error, rather, it treats them as interacting: reasoning failures most often surface as incomplete/poorly retrieved knowledge, and structured reasoning support is best understood as a way of helping clinicians deploy the knowledge they already have more completely and more self-critically, not as a substitute for acquiring it. 


This paper proposes the Adversarial Reasoning Method (ARM), an eight-step framework for clinical diagnostic reasoning organized around the steps Observe, Represent, Compete, Attack, Challenge, Update, Reflect, and Integrate. Five of these steps are, by the paper’s own account, procedural restatements of previously published work (specifically Bordage, Kassirer, and Croskerry). ARM’s actual claim to contribution is narrower and is stated as such throughout: sequencing those elements into one connected procedure, converting a situational debiasing tool into a mandatory step, and closing the loop with explicit Reflect and Integrate stages; extending the tradition of deliberate reflection (Mamede, Schmidt, & Rikers, 2007; Mamede & Schmidt) into a fixed position within a whole-case sequence rather than proposing it as an independent intervention. 


The framework is presented explicitly as a conceptual proposal. It has not been empirically tested, and no claim of superiority over existing models of clinical reasoning is made. This paper also proposes a small-scale case-series pilot as a feasibility step preceding any larger validation study, and addresses two practical concerns often absent from clinical-reasoning frameworks: the conflict between procedural rigor, clinical throughput, and case volume; and the operational constraints of introducing structured adversarial documentation into real clinical and educational settings.

The Eight Steps


1. Observe: Structured intake of clinical/contextual data; separation of observation from interpretation


2. Represent: Compression of findings into a concise, pattern-oriented summary (externalization tool)


3. Compete: Generation of competing hypotheses with supporting/contradicting evidence


4. Attack: Adversarial audit of the leading hypothesis via structured disconfirming questions


5. Challenge: Test selection based on expected impact on the leading decision, not breadth of hypotheses moved


6. Update: Iterative revision as new data accumulate


7. Reflect: Structured, socially embedded post-case self-evaluation


8. Integrate: Long-term pattern formation across cases


—


ARM is best described as a synthesis and procedural sequencing of existing, separately published models, with the Attack step’s mandatory placement and the Reflect and Integrate stages as its only structurally original elements.
